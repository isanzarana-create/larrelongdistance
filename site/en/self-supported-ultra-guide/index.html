Running
self-supported

What it means to run an ultra-trail without assistance, how much water and food you need, when to sleep and how to prepare for a 200 km distance.

What a self-supported ultra-trail is

A self-supported ultra-trail is a race in which every runner carries everything they will need. There are no aid stations every fifteen kilometres, no drop bag, no crew car waiting at the top of the climb. There are checkpoints, yes, but they are control and stamping points: they confirm that you passed through, they do not feed you.

The difference from a classic ultra is not just logistics. It changes what you decide. In a supported ultra, the key decision is pace. In a self-supported one, the key decision is resource management: how much weight you carry, where you refill water, when you eat, when you sleep. Almost nobody drops out of a race like this for lack of legs; they drop out because they miscalculated the water, the food or the cold.

Water is the first problem

Water weighs a kilo per litre and you cannot carry all of it. So planning is done not by kilometres but by stretches between reliable water points: village fountains, bars, cemeteries, sports centres. They are marked on the map before the start, with an alternative in case one is dry or closed at night.

As a working reference, in mid-mountain terrain you drink around 400–500 ml per hour in mild temperatures and 600–800 ml per hour in the heat. Between two points five hours apart, that is more than two litres. A water filter or purification tablets weigh very little and turn any stream into an option; livestock troughs, no.

In autumn, in the Álava ranges, the trap is the opposite of what everyone expects: it is cool, you drink less than you think and you arrive dehydrated without ever having felt hot.

Food: calculate by hours, not by kilometres

In long races most runners sustain between 200 and 300 kcal per hour, and almost never more. In an event that can last forty or fifty hours, that is many thousands of calories: more than fit in a reasonable pack. So the question is not “how much food do I carry” but “how much food do I carry on me and where does the rest come from”.

What works: alternating sweet and savoury before your stomach shuts down, combining solids with liquids, carrying something hot or salty for the small hours and having two or three options you know you can eat even when nauseous. What does not work: trying a gel, a bar or a flavour in the race that you have not tested on long training days.

Each race sets out in its rules whether you may buy in shops along the route or refill at public fountains, and that completely changes the food plan. It is the first thing to read. For Ultra Trail Larre the essentials are summarised in the rules.

Sleeping, or not sleeping

An event with a fifty-hour time limit spans two nights. For most runners, not sleeping at all for forty-odd hours means reaching the second small hours with hallucinations, disorientation and bad decisions on terrain where decisions matter.

The three strategies actually used: not sleeping (only if you have already tried it in training and know how your head responds), micro-naps of ten to twenty minutes when the first serious low arrives, or a planned stop of one to two hours at a point decided in advance. The difference between it working and not working is having decided before the start, not at four in the morning lying in a ditch.

The gear that really matters

Beyond what each organiser requires, there is a core that is not up for debate in self-supported racing:

  • Headlamp with good battery life and a spare set of batteries or battery pack. Two nights are a lot of hours of light.
  • Waterproof jacket with a hood and a dry thermal layer, kept for the night or for a stop.
  • Charged phone, power bank and cables. It is your map, your emergency phone and your backup torch.
  • Water capacity of 1.5–2 litres, plus a filter or tablets.
  • Survival blanket and whistle. They weigh nothing and keep you alive while help arrives.
  • Minimal foot kit: tape, small scissors, a needle, something for blisters. A badly treated blister retires more people than a climb.
  • Gloves and a hat or neck gaiter. The cold at dawn on a ridge is nothing like the cold at the start.

The mandatory gear list for Ultra Trail Larre will be published with the final rules of the 1st Edition. When it is ready it will have its own page and will be announced on @ultratraillarre.

Navigating with a GPS track

In self-supported races the route is not marked with ribbons: you follow an official GPS track. That means navigation is part of the event and is trained like a climb.

In practice: load the track on your watch and on your phone, with the area maps downloaded for use without coverage; mark the checkpoints as points of interest; know what to do when the watch warns you that you are off-track (stop, go back to the last safe point, do not improvise shortcuts at night); and do not depend on a single battery for the one thing that tells you where you are.

Zero waste: how to run without leaving anything

At Ultra Trail Larre, dropping a single piece of litter means immediate disqualification, and it is the only rule in the regulations with no appeal. In practice it comes down to three habits: your own rubbish bag in a fixed pocket, always the same one; gels and bars with the tab tied or stowed the moment you open them, never “later”; and decanting what you can into reusable bags before the start, so you carry less wrapping from the beginning.

How to prepare for a 200 km distance

With a year ahead, preparation falls into three blocks. This is not a personalised training plan (that depends on your history and, if there are health doubts, on a professional), but it is the structure shared by almost everyone who reaches the finish.

  • Base · months 1 to 4Accumulated volume and climbing, at a comfortable pace. Lots of time on your feet, little intensity. This builds the capacity to endure hours, not speed.
  • Specific · months 5 to 8Long back-to-back outings (Saturday and Sunday in a row), at least two complete night outings, all with the pack loaded to real race weight. You train your stomach by eating on the move what you will eat on race day.
  • Sharpening · months 9 to 10One long simulation of 12 to 24 hours with the final gear, the final food and footwear test, and then real rest. Whatever is not tested by now is not debuted in the race.

Two sessions are non-negotiable: running at night, for several hours, until not seeing clearly is normal; and chaining two long days in a row, to learn how the body responds when it starts with tired legs. And there are two parts that almost nobody trains and that decide races: the stomach and the feet.

Ten mistakes that keep repeating

  • Starting too fast in the first four hours because the pack does not weigh much yet.
  • Debuting gear, footwear or nutrition on race day.
  • Calculating water by kilometres rather than by time between reliable points.
  • Having no sleep plan and deciding it in the small hours, with no judgement left.
  • Carrying a single headlamp, or half-used spare batteries.
  • Stopping eating as soon as nausea appears, instead of changing texture or flavour.
  • Not treating the first discomfort in a foot to avoid losing ten minutes.
  • Trusting a single battery for the GPS.
  • Carrying the bare minimum of warm clothing because it was nice at the start.
  • Not having read the rules until the week before.

Frequently asked questions

Can you run a 200 km ultra without having done a 100 first?

You can, but the chance of finishing drops a lot. What a 100 km or 24-hour event gives you is not the kilometres: it is having spent a night running, having handled a serious low and knowing what your stomach will eat at hour twenty. If there is no long ultra behind you, the reasonable substitute is your own outings of 15 to 24 hours on similar terrain.

How much should the pack weigh?

In real self-sufficiency, between three and six kilos with water, depending on the weather and the sleep strategy. Below three usually means something important is missing; above six, that you are carrying fear in the form of weight.

What happens if I decide to withdraw halfway?

In a self-supported race a withdrawal is always reported to the organisation; nobody drops out silently: until they know, the control centre assumes you are still in the mountains. The specific Ultra Trail Larre procedure (pick-up points and phone number) will be part of the final rules and of the compulsory briefing the day before.

Is Ultra Trail Larre a good first self-supported race?

Honestly, no. It is a good second or third step. To start in the format, a 50 to 100 km event with no assistance, or your own two-day route, makes more sense. What Ultra Trail Larre is, is a place where self-sufficiency is not decoration on the poster: 226.9 km, zero waste and a track with you, the mountain and your brevet.
